OUR STORY

About Us

Why a group of Richmond churches gathers every September to lament, repent, and forgive — together.

How We Began

One Day, One Step began in 2022 as the “Day of Repentance,” an event intended to foster racial healing through lament, confession, repentance, and forgiveness. The service was held at a church on Monument Avenue, near the site where the Robert E. Lee statue stood for 131 years. Following the death of George Floyd and the unrest that followed, that statue became a public gathering place for raising awareness of racial injustice and inequity — even though the land it stood on was donated by a church, and the statue itself dedicated in a service led by a Presbyterian pastor in 1890. In an effort to seek peace and healing, a steering committee arranged the 2022 Day of Repentance during the anniversary month of the statue’s removal.

Based on the response to that first gathering, the steering committee decided to pursue an annual service of repentance. In 2023 the name changed to “One Day, One Step” (ODOS) — a fitting name, since the first Christians were known as followers of the “odos” (ὁδός), the Greek word for “way” (cf. Acts 9:2). We believe that all who follow the way of Jesus are called to the liturgical rhythms of lament, confession, repentance, and forgiveness. The event is especially designed for those committed to actively promoting racial healing in Richmond, Virginia.

Our Theology

The One Day, One Step Steering Committee is composed of pastors and lay leaders from churches across the Richmond metro region, from a variety of denominations. While we hold different theological perspectives on a number of secondary issues, we are unified in our faith as articulated in the Apostles’ Creed, and together we rely on God’s redeeming grace to heal the divisions in our city.

Why Lament?

Lament is the spiritual discipline of acknowledging and sitting with suffering that has been experienced personally or communally. Scripture connects lament to personal trauma in the story of Job: after losing everything, Job laments by crying out to God, tearing his robe, and shaving his head (cf. Job 1). Before rationalizing what happened, or trying to fix it, he takes a moment to feel and to grieve. Scripture also connects lament to communal suffering — the book of Lamentations is Jeremiah’s meditation on the destruction of Judah, and almost a third of the Psalms are laments.

Lament is a personal discipline as well as an institutional one. Institutionally, it means creating space to acknowledge the stories of those who have experienced harm from the sins of racism and racial oppression. Lament resists the temptation to either ignore painful realities as a defense mechanism, or to deny them as a form of self-exoneration. It begins with acknowledging the reality of historical trauma and contemporary pain long enough to feel it — long enough for it to move from abstraction to embodiment. Truth-telling is a prerequisite for healing.

Why Repent?

We understand biblical repentance to mean “turning from sin and toward a godly lifestyle.” The Old Testament verb for repent is the Hebrew shuv, a turning from sin and evil that is often corporate (cf. Ezekiel 18:30). The Greek word most often used for repentance in the New Testament is metanoia — not just a change in action, but a change in mind, heart, attitude, and prejudice. When Paul uses this word, he implies that God cares not only about our external actions, but also our hearts, and that authentic inward change should affect our behavior.

Some may wonder why we repent of sin that happened in the past. Scripture repeatedly speaks of the iniquity of one generation visiting the next (e.g. Exodus 20:5), and community leaders confessing not just personal sin but ancestral sin: “We and our fathers have sinned” (Daniel 9:8, Psalm 106:7). Nehemiah grieves and repents for the actions of his fellow Israelites, though he did not commit those sins himself (Neh. 1:6–7).

We are not free-floating individuals disconnected from the past — we are shaped by both the glory and the rebellion of those who came before us. While we may not have committed the historic sins of racism and racial oppression, we often share in their communal repercussions. God invites us, in grace, to take responsibility for and turn away from how individuals, communities, and systems have harmed others in the past, and to work to repair the ongoing effects of those harms.

Why Forgive?

When Jesus is asked how many times we are to forgive someone, he replies, “I do not say to you seven times, but seventy times seven” (cf. Matthew 18:22). Scholars don’t believe Jesus means literally 490 times, but that we should continue forgiving until the forgiveness is complete. As God’s forgiveness of us is unlimited, we should extend the same unlimited forgiveness to others.

When offering forgiveness for egregious offenses, such as racial trauma, we acknowledge that we are limited in our human capacity to offer complete forgiveness. It is only when we turn our hearts to God and rely on the Holy Spirit to fill us with God’s infinite grace that we can begin to release the forgiveness that leads to true and complete healing — as individuals, as a corporate body, and as a city.

What Does This Have to Do With Healing?

We believe that God heals and redeems. As 2 Chronicles 7:14 says: “If my people who are called by my name will humble themselves and pray and seek my face and turn from their wicked ways, then I will hear from heaven, and forgive their sin and heal their land.”

One Day, One Step is an opportunity for the Christian community of Richmond to put that scripture into action. During the service we explain and embody biblically rooted lament, confession, repentance, and forgiveness. Confession is an act of faith that the Gospel is more powerful than our collective sin and shame; repentance affirms that racism does not have to have the last word in Richmond’s story. We believe repentance, through the Holy Spirit, must change how we live and love one another — which is why One Day, One Step partners with Richmond organizations to host a Repair Fair immediately following the service, so residents can learn how to tangibly “seek the welfare of the city” (cf. Jeremiah 29:7). We believe One Day, One Step and its partners can play a real role in how God brings structural repair to Richmond and interpersonal healing to its residents.
